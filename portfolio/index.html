    Technical details

    Leggio translates with the AI models built into Chrome, so the words you select are translated on your own computer, for free. You can plug in your own API key if you’d rather use a different model.

    Nothing goes to a server: no account, no analytics, no ads. Saved words stay in your browser.

    • Chrome built-in AI
    • On-device
    • Context-aware
    • Web pages + PDFs

    Inspiration

    Reading in a language you are learning means stopping at every word you do not know, switching to a dictionary or a translation, and losing the sentence on the way back. I wanted the meaning to arrive beside the word, in the sentence it belongs to, so the reading never stops.

      The dictionary has an assistant, powered by Claude, that answers questions in plain language: how to say “have you eaten?”, what a word means, or what tone sandhi is. It sits beside search, so a learner who doesn’t know the spelling can still find the word.

      Inspiration

      Fuzhounese is the dialect my grandparents mainly speak, and there was no English dictionary of it online. I wanted a place to keep its words and the voices that say them, and to make it something anyone could learn from.

      Technical details

      Footprints, roof heights and years come from NYC Open Data. Satellite and 3D views come from Google’s Photorealistic 3D Tiles, and archive photos open on Urban Archive.

      The record started with researched entries for buildings in every Manhattan neighborhood, drawn from Wikipedia and the city’s building historians. Anyone signed in can add to it.

      • Cited sources

      Inspiration

      Walk past almost any building in New York and there is no way to find out what happened inside it. I wanted a map where every building has a record: what it was, who was there, and what it saw.

      GREATERCIRCLE

      Visit

      Where would your flight go, if it could not go there?

      A globe for anyone curious about their flight path. Click two points, or type two places, for the great-circle distance; then name the countries to stay out of, and it finds the exact shortest route around them. For enthusiasts, and for passengers whose seat-back map is out of date, or drawing a line across a war zone the plane will never cross.

      Technical details

      The route is the classical shortest path among polygonal obstacles, carried onto the sphere: a chain of great-circle arcs that bends only at the avoided countries’ convex corners, found by A* over their tangent visibility graph, in three-dimensional unit vectors. No grid, no buffer: exact for the drawn 1:50m borders, and settled in a few hundred milliseconds in a Web Worker.

      Borders from Natural Earth; places from GeoNames and OurAirports, with Photon for the long tail. The globe is a canvas with its own projector, a few milliseconds a frame at any zoom.

      • Exact shortest paths
      • 241 countries
      • 14,500 places
      • Web Worker

      Inspiration

      Seat-back flight maps often show an old route, a straight line, or a path across a country the plane will never enter. I wanted to see where a flight can really go once part of the map is closed to it.
