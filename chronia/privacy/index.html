Privacy policy

CHRONIA is an atlas run by one person, and it is free to use. It is paid for by small advertisements from Google, which readers without Premium see beside the date, and by Premium, an optional one-time payment (section 7). Google’s advertisements may use cookies, as section 7 describes; nothing else here measures you for anyone else. This policy sets out everything the atlas collects, why, and what you can do about it.

Effective 25 November 2026. See also the terms of use.

1The short version

Looking at the atlas collects nothing that names you: it adds one to a running count of visits for the day, and Vercel, which hosts the atlas, counts the pages you open, where you came from, your country, and your kind of browser and device, with no cookies and nothing that follows you to other sites. Opening a card for a state, a people, or a city may ask an AI model to write a short note about it, which sends only what the atlas shows about that subject, never anything about you. You can sign in with Google from the account button at the top right, and after a minute on the atlas it offers once, in a small note you can turn down; signing in keeps the name and email address of that account, and it is needed to report an error on the map, which keeps them with the report, and to make a timelapse video, which the atlas makes in your own browser and does not keep or send anywhere. Signed in, you can also choose a username and a picture and comment on a country’s card; these are public to anyone, and a picture is checked by an AI model before it is kept (section 8). The map layers you turn on and off are remembered in your own browser. Readers without Premium see advertisements from Google, which may set cookies to choose and measure them; in the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom, and Switzerland, Google asks first. UCHRONIA, the map maker that lives alongside the atlas, keeps your maps in your browser and sends a request to Anthropic’s Claude only when you ask its historian for something (section 17). Ask, and I will delete anything the atlas holds about you.

3Looking at the atlas

The atlas itself sets no cookies; the advertisements, which readers without Premium see, come from Google, and section 7 says what Google does to show them. The atlas measures its own use in two ways. The first is a count of visits. When a page opens and your browser has not opened one here in the last thirty minutes, it asks the atlas’s database to add one to the day’s total. The database keeps only the date and that number: not your address, the page, where you came from, or anything else about the visit. To tell a new visit from the next page of the same one, your browser keeps the time it last opened a page here (see section 9). The second is Vercel Web Analytics, run by Vercel, the company that hosts the atlas. For each page you open on chroniaatlas.com, it records the time, the page’s address without anything after it (so not the date or place on the map you are looking at), the site you came from, your country and region, and the name and version of your browser and system and whether you are on a phone, tablet, or computer. It sets no cookies and stores no IP address; to count one visitor once, Vercel makes a code from your request that it throws away after a day, and it never links you to other sites. I see only the totals, never one visitor’s pages. The page for listing reports is not measured this way.

To show you the atlas, a few services necessarily receive the ordinary request your browser makes, which includes your IP address and the kind of browser you use: Vercel, which hosts the atlas and its typefaces; Wikipedia and Wikimedia, from which a card loads the opening of an article and its picture; Wikidata, from which a card loads a flag and the languages spoken; and Amazon Web Services, which hosts the open Terrain Tiles behind the shaded relief. None of them is asked to identify you, and none of them reports anything back to me. Each handles those requests under its own privacy policy.

4The notes on the cards

When you open the card for a state, a people, or a city, the atlas may ask an AI model to write a short note about it as it was at that date. The request carries only what the atlas shows about the subject: its name, the date, and facts such as its ruler, its capital, and its neighbours, or, for a city, where it is on the map. It goes to a small program of mine on Supabase, which passes it to DeepSeek, a model made by the Chinese company DeepSeek, or, when DeepSeek can’t answer, to Claude, a model made by Anthropic. DeepSeek and Anthropic each receive it as a request from my program, under their own privacy policies, not from you; Anthropic’s commercial terms do not let it train its models on what it is sent. The program keeps the note that comes back and shows that same note to anyone who opens the same card later. Nothing about you is stored with it, and no sign-in is needed.

The daily count. To keep the notes within a daily budget, the program counts how many new notes each visitor asks for in a day, under a code made from your IP address, the date, and a secret, which cannot be turned back into the address. Each count is a date, that code, and a number. Your IP address itself is not stored, and each day’s counts are deleted the first time a note is written on a later day.

5Reporting an error

Anyone can report an error on the map after signing in with Google. The sign-in button and its window are Google’s own, loaded from Google when you open the report panel or the account panel while signed out, or when the atlas offers you a sign-in after a minute, and the account itself is kept by Supabase, the service that runs the atlas’s database. When you sign in, Google passes the atlas your name, email address, profile picture, and Google account identifier.

A report keeps the name and email address of the account it came from, the place and dates you chose, what the map showed there, the map layers that were on, your note, my reply, and when it was sent and closed. I can read all of it; the people who help me check the map can read the reports and replies, but not the email addresses. Nobody else sees a report. To keep the reports within reason, each account can send up to 50 a day, counted from the reports themselves.

The page that lists the reports is opened with a password. Wrong passwords are counted for a day under a code made from the IP address, the date, and a secret, as for the notes, and deleted after two days.

6Opening a map in UCHRONIA

The save panel can open the map you are looking at in UCHRONIA, a separate map maker of mine. The atlas draws that map in your browser and puts the whole of it in the link, after the # sign, a part of an address that browsers never send to a server. Nothing about it passes through the atlas. What UCHRONIA does once it opens is described in section 17.

7Advertisements and Premium

CHRONIA Premium is a single payment of $10, made while signed in, that takes the advertisements off the atlas and the watermark off its timelapse videos and saved images for that account, gives it five times the daily notes and UCHRONIA historian questions, and puts your reports of errors first in the queue; the people who read the reports see that a report comes from an account with Premium. You pay on Stripe’s own page, under its privacy policy; the atlas never sees your card. Stripe is sent your account’s email address, to fill in its form, and the atlas keeps your account’s identifier, Stripe’s references for the payment, its amount, and its date, for as long as the account has Premium. Your browser remembers that the account has Premium, so the page need not ask each time, and forgets it when you sign out.

The advertisements. Readers without Premium see up to two small banners at the top of the atlas’s own page, chosen and served by Google AdSense; the other pages, these included, carry none. To show them, your browser loads Google’s code, which receives your IP address, the page’s address, and your browser’s details, and may set or read cookies and similar identifiers to choose the advertisements, limit how often you see each, count and measure them, and prevent fraud; Google may choose them by what it knows of you. In the European Economic Area, the United Kingdom, and Switzerland, Google’s own consent message asks you first; anywhere, you can turn off personalised advertisements in Google’s Ad Settings. Google sets out what it does with this information in How Google uses information from sites or apps that use our services. From Google the atlas receives only totals of views, clicks, and earnings, never anything about you. Signed in with Premium, the page loads none of Google’s advertising code; signed in without it, the page waits to hear from my program whether the account has Premium before it shows any.

8Profiles and comments

Signed in with Google, you can comment on a country’s card, reply to comments, and upvote them. To do so you make a profile: a username you choose and, if you like, a picture. Making one asks you to tick a box saying that you are 13 or older and old enough in your country to use it without a parent’s consent (section 15). The atlas keeps the time you ticked it; it never asks for your age or your date of birth, and keeps neither.

What is kept. A profile keeps the username, the picture’s file, when the profile was made and the username last changed, how many times the picture was changed that day, and whether the account has been blocked. Each comment keeps its text, the name of the country it was written under, the map’s date its writer was looking at, and when it was sent; a reply also keeps which comment it answers. Each upvote keeps which account gave it, to which comment, and when, and a profile keeps how many upvotes it gave or took back that day, for the daily limit. Each report of a comment or a picture keeps who made it, what it reports, and when. All of it is tied to the account you signed in with (section 5) by the account’s identifier, not by your name or email address.

What is public. Usernames, pictures, and comments are public. Anyone who opens a country’s card can read its comments, each with its writer’s username and picture and the map’s date it was written at, whether they are signed in or not, and a picture can be opened by anyone who has its address. So are replies, under the comment they answer, and the number of upvotes each comment has. Your name and email address from Google are never shown with them, and other readers never see who upvoted or who reported what: an upvote is stored with your account, but only its count is shown. The list of your own comments in the Account panel, which also shows any of yours that are hidden from others, is shown only to you.

The picture check. The page cuts a picture you choose to a square of 256 by 256 pixels and saves it afresh, which leaves behind the details a camera or phone writes into a file, such as where and when it was taken. It then goes to my program on Supabase, which sends it to Claude, Anthropic’s model, to ask whether it suits a public profile on a history site read by people of all ages. Anthropic receives it as a request from my program, under the commercial terms that section 4 describes. Only a picture Claude accepts is stored, in Supabase’s storage under a random name; a picture it refuses, or one that can’t be checked, is not stored, and you are asked to choose another or try again later.

The filters and the reports. Before anything is stored, my program checks usernames against public lists of offensive and reserved words, and comments against a short list of slurs and obscenities and for web addresses; text it refuses is not kept. A signed-in reader can report a comment or a picture. A comment or a picture reported by three different readers is hidden until someone on the team looks at it. I and the people who help me check the map can see every reported, hidden, or removed comment and picture, with the writer’s username and account identifier and the number of reports, and a reviewer can put it back, remove it, or block the account; a blocked account’s comments leave the cards, and it can no longer post, report, or change its profile.

Deleting. You can delete any comment of yours with Delete under it; the comment, its upvotes, and the reports of it are erased. If others have replied to it, its text is erased and a “Deleted comment”, without your username or picture, is left in its place, so that the replies under it keep their thread; once the last of them is deleted, that place is erased too. Pressing an upvote again takes it back and erases it. Changing your picture, or removing it with Edit profile, deletes the old file. To have your profile, picture, comments, upvotes, and the reports you made erased altogether, ask me as section 13 describes; others’ replies to your comments then stay, under a “Deleted comment” in their place, as they do when you delete one.

9What stays in your browser

Some things are kept in your own browser’s storage so the atlas remembers them next time: which map layers you turned on or off, whether the key and the wars panel are open, and whether you chose the flat map; when you last said Not now to the offer to sign in, so it isn’t made again for thirty days; the time you last opened a page, for the visit count; whether the account you are signed in with has Premium; the address of your profile picture, if you have one, with the account it belongs to, so the account button can show it at once; and, if you have signed in, the token that keeps you signed in, with the place on the map to come back to after Google’s sign-in. A comment you are writing is kept in your browser until you post it or close the tab, so that signing in again does not lose it; it is sent nowhere until you post it.

Google’s advertising code, where it runs, keeps its own cookies and identifiers, as section 7 describes; they are Google’s, and its consent message and Ad Settings govern them. The rest of this information stays on your device. It is not sent to me or to anyone else, and clearing your browser’s data for chroniaatlas.com removes all of it. The sign-in token is the one exception in kind: your browser presents it to Supabase to prove it is you, which is all it is for.

Your account and your reports are kept because you chose to sign in and send them; that choice is your consent, and you can withdraw it at any time by asking me to delete them. Your profile and your comments are kept, and shown, for the same reason: you chose to make and post them. The picture check, the word filters, the reports, and keeping a removed comment or picture for the team rest on my legitimate interest in keeping the comments safe and free of abuse for everyone who reads them. The daily counts for the notes and the passwords rest on my legitimate interest in keeping the atlas within its budget and safe from abuse, and the visit count and Vercel’s page-view totals on my interest in knowing, roughly, how many people use the atlas and how they find it. The advertisements rest on your consent where the law requires it, which Google’s consent message asks for, and elsewhere on my interest in paying for the atlas, against which you can turn off personalised advertisements or buy Premium. A Premium payment is kept because it is a purchase you made.

12How long things are kept

The daily visit totals are kept as a running count of how the atlas is used. Vercel keeps its page-view records for the time its plan allows and shows me only totals. The notes are kept for as long as CHRONIA exists; they hold nothing about anyone who asked for them. The daily counts for the notes are deleted soon after their day ends, and the counts of wrong passwords after two days. Reports, and the account they came from, are kept as a record of the map’s corrections for as long as CHRONIA exists, or until you ask me to delete them; when an account is deleted, everything tied to it is deleted with it. A profile, its picture, its comments and replies, and its upvotes are kept until you delete them or ask me to. A comment a reviewer removes is kept out of sight, for the team, so that the removal can be reviewed and undone; a picture hidden by reports, or removed by a reviewer, is moved at once to storage that only the team can see, and a removed one is kept there for 30 days, for the same reason, and its file is deleted soon after that, whether or not anyone on the team opens its list. A comment you delete, its upvotes, and the reports of it are erased at once, apart from a “Deleted comment” with no text or name kept in its place while others’ replies are under it (section 8).

14Security

The atlas is served over HTTPS. Its database lets a browser add to the visit count but never read it back. Reports, profiles, and comments reach it only through programs of mine that check who is signed in, no browser can read or write their tables directly, and email addresses are shown to no one but me. Only my program can add a picture to the storage that holds them. No system is perfectly secure; if something went wrong that affected your information, I would tell you.

Profiles and comments are for people aged 13 and over, or older where a country sets a higher age for agreeing to a service like this without a parent (16 in some of Europe); making a profile asks you to say that you are, though it does not check. If you are a parent or guardian and believe your child has signed in or made a profile, write to me at noahlee519@gmail.com, with the username if you know it, and I will erase the profile and everything posted under it, and delete the account.

17Using UCHRONIA

Your map. The map you draw in UCHRONIA stays in your own browser and goes nowhere unless you send it somewhere. If you save it as a file, it stays wherever you put it. A share link carries the whole map inside the link, after the # sign, so anyone you give the link to can open your map, and no one else can see it. UCHRONIA’s visits are added to the atlas’s daily count (section 3), but its page is not measured by Vercel Web Analytics. When you zoom in on its satellite view, the sharper imagery comes from NASA’s Global Imagery Browse Services, which receives the ordinary request your browser makes, as the services in section 3 do.

Signing in. UCHRONIA uses the same Google sign-in and the same account as the rest of the atlas (section 5). Signing in is needed only for the historian, so that each person’s daily allowance can be counted. UCHRONIA does not create a profile for you or show anything about you to anyone.

The historian. The Historian menu asks Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, to write a history of your map, redraw your map from a turning point, or answer your questions about the world it has written. Each request sends what you typed, a written list of the ways your map differs from the real world, and, if you are asking a question, the history together with the questions and answers before it. All of this goes first to a small program of mine on Supabase that holds the key to Anthropic’s service, then to Anthropic, which writes the reply and sends it back to your browser. My program keeps nothing you send and nothing that comes back. Anthropic receives it as a request from my program, under its privacy policy and the commercial terms that section 4 describes, which do not let it train its models on what it is sent; it may keep requests for a limited time to watch for misuse. My program checks that you are signed in, but it does not store what you send with your account, and it does not pass your name or email address to Anthropic. Even so, please don’t put anything personal into a request.

The daily count. Each account may make five requests a day, or twenty-five with Premium; a history, a redrawn map with its history, and a question each count as one. My program keeps the count under a code made from your account identifier and a secret, which cannot be turned back into the identifier. Each count is only a date, that code, and a number, and counts older than thirty days are deleted. To know which allowance applies, the program checks whether your account has Premium (section 7). These counts rest on my legitimate interest in keeping the historian within its budget.

What stays in your browser. Your map, the one you had before opening a shared map, your view settings, the histories written for you and the conversations beneath them (up to the most recent thirty), whether your account has Premium, and, if you have signed in, the token that keeps you signed in. None of it is kept anywhere else, and clearing your browser’s data for chroniaatlas.com removes it.
