Privacy policy

Noah Darwin Lee back

This is my website. I made it, I run it, and I’m the only one here. Below is every single thing the site collects, why it does, and how to make it stop.

Effective 22 November 2026. See also the terms of use.

1. The short version

If you just read the site, I learn nothing about you. Your visit adds one to a count of visits for the day, and that count is just a number: no address, no name, no page. Opening citylayoutguessr adds one anonymous line a day saying that some browser opened the game. If you sign in to the game, I store your name, email address, and profile picture so your name and score can go on the leaderboard. Nobody sees your email address but me. If you write in the message box on the front page, I get what you wrote and when, and that’s it. UCHRONIA’s historian uses the same Google sign-in. When you ask it for a history, what you type and a description of your map go to Anthropic so it can write back. I don’t keep any of that, just a count of how many requests each account made that day. If you want any of it gone, ask me and I’ll delete it. CHRONIA, my atlas at chroniaatlas.com, has its own privacy policy.

2. Who I am

“I” and “me” on this page mean me, Noah Darwin Lee. I run noahdarwinlee.com and every page under it (the “site”), and I decide what it collects and why. In data protection terms, that makes me the controller of everything described here. There’s no company behind this, just me. If you have a question about anything on this page, email me at noahlee519@gmail.com.

3. Reading the site

The pages are plain files. No cookies, no analytics, no ads. The only thing I measure is how many visits the whole site gets. When you open a page and your browser hasn’t opened one here in the last thirty minutes, it asks the site’s database to add one to today’s total. The database keeps the date and that number. Not your address, not the page, not where you came from, nothing else. So it can tell a new visit apart from you clicking to the next page, your browser remembers when it last opened a page here (see section 9).

A few services have to see the ordinary request your browser makes just to show you the site, and that request includes your IP address and what kind of browser you use. GitHub Pages hosts the site and its typeface. Google Fonts serves the type in the blog’s mail window and in GREATERCIRCLE. One tool loads drawing libraries from cdnjs, which Cloudflare runs. When you zoom in on the satellite views in UCHRONIA and GREATERCIRCLE, the sharper imagery comes from NASA’s Global Imagery Browse Services, and UCHRONIA’s shaded relief comes from the open Terrain Tiles that Amazon Web Services hosts. Drill looks up each figure’s picture on Wikipedia and loads it from Wikimedia. I don’t ask any of them to figure out who you are, and none of them report anything back to me. Each one handles those requests under its own privacy policy.

4. Playing citylayoutguessr

The visit counter. The first time your browser opens the game on a given day, the game writes down one line saying a visit happened: what kind of event it was, the date, the page address, and the page you came from, if your browser tells it. It’s written once per browser per day. There’s no IP address and no name in it. If you’re signed in, it also has your account identifier. If you’re not, nothing in it points to you.

Everything else. Your round in progress, your settings, your colours, and how you did on today’s puzzle all stay in your own browser (see section 9). None of it gets sent anywhere.

5. Signing in

You don’t have to sign in. In the game, signing in puts your name next to your score on the leaderboard. In UCHRONIA, you need to sign in to use the historian, so I can count everyone’s daily allowance. Either way you sign in with your Google account, through Supabase, which runs the game’s database and sign-in. In UCHRONIA, the sign-in button and its window are Google’s own, loaded from Google when you open the historian while signed out. It’s the same account in both places, but UCHRONIA doesn’t make you a profile or show anything about you to anyone.

What I get. When you sign in, Google gives the site your name, email address, profile picture, and Google account identifier. Supabase stores that as your account. The game copies your name and picture into a profile, and you can swap the name for whatever display name you like.

What everyone can see. Your display name, your profile picture, your daily scores, and where you are on the two leaderboards. Anyone who opens the game can see those.

What they can’t. Your email address. It’s stored with your account, and only I can see it, and only through the database’s own dashboard.

6. Scores

When you finish the daily challenge while signed in, the game records one line: your account identifier, the date, how many of the ten you got right, how long it took you, and when it was posted. One line per person per day. Those lines are the leaderboards, so they’re public in the way section 5 describes. Custom games never get posted.

7. Leaving feedback

The front page and the art page each have a box where you can tell me what you think of the site. When you send something, I store what you wrote, which of the two pages you sent it from, and when it arrived, in a Supabase database. That’s all. No name, no email address, no account, unless you type one of them into the message yourself. Supabase sees the ordinary request your browser makes, like the services in section 3 do, but I don’t keep anything from it with the message. Only I can read what you send. The site can add a message, but it can never read one back.

8. Using UCHRONIA

Your map. The map you draw stays in your own browser (see section 9) and doesn’t go anywhere unless you send it somewhere. If you save it as a file, it stays wherever you put it. A share link carries the whole map inside the link, after the # sign, which is a part of an address browsers never send to a server. Anyone you give the link to can open your map, and nobody else can see it.

The historian. The Historian menu asks Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, to write a history of your map, redraw your map from a turning point, or answer your questions about the world it wrote. Each request sends what you typed, a written list of how your map differs from the real world, and, if you’re asking a question, the history plus the questions and answers before it. All that goes first to a small program of mine on Supabase that holds the key to Anthropic’s service, then on to Anthropic, which writes the reply and sends it back to your browser. My program doesn’t keep anything you send or anything that comes back. Anthropic gets it as a request from a business customer and handles it under its privacy policy and commercial terms, which don’t let it train its models on what you send. It may keep requests for a limited time to watch for misuse. My program checks that you’re signed in, but it doesn’t store what you send with your account, and it doesn’t pass your name or email address to Anthropic. Still, don’t put anything personal in there.

The daily count. The historian runs on a daily budget, so each account gets five requests a day. A history, a redrawn map with its history, and a question each count as one. My program counts them under a code made from your account identifier and a secret, and that code can’t be turned back into the identifier. Each count is just a date, the code, and a number, and I delete counts older than thirty days.

Saved histories. The histories written for you, and the conversations under them, stay in your browser, up to the last thirty. They aren’t kept anywhere else.

9. What stays in your browser

Some things are saved in your own browser’s storage so the site remembers them next time. Everywhere: whether you picked day or night, and when you last opened a page, for the visit count. In the game: a round in progress, your custom setup, your colours, the leaderboard tab you last opened, your result on today’s puzzle and whether it’s been posted, the date of your last visit, and, if you’ve signed in, the token that keeps you signed in. In the flash card tool: your lists, drafts, and colours. On the blog: which letters you’ve read or starred and how you sized the panes. In UCHRONIA: your map, the one you had before opening a shared map, your view settings, your saved histories, and, if you’ve signed in, the token that keeps you signed in to the historian. In Drill: your progress and settings in each deck, and the addresses of the figures’ pictures, for a week.

All of this stays on your device. It doesn’t get sent to me or anyone else, and clearing your browser’s data for noahdarwinlee.com wipes it. The sign-in tokens are the one kind of exception: your browser shows them to Supabase to prove it’s you, and that’s all they’re for.

10. Why I’m allowed to

Where the law asks for a legal basis, here are mine. I keep your account, profile, and scores because you chose to sign in and post them. That choice is your consent, and you can take it back whenever you want by asking me to delete your account. I keep a feedback message because you chose to send it. The game’s daily visit counter rests on my legitimate interest in knowing, roughly, whether anyone plays the game, and it does that without identifying anyone who isn’t signed in. UCHRONIA’s daily counts rest on my legitimate interest in keeping the historian within its budget, and the site’s visit count on my interest in knowing, roughly, how many people read the site.

11. Who else is involved

Besides the services in section 3, three more are involved. Supabase runs the database and sign-in for the game and the historian, keeps the messages from the feedback box, and runs the program that passes UCHRONIA’s requests to the historian and keeps its daily counts. The game’s copy of Supabase’s script is delivered by jsDelivr. Google handles the sign-in itself. Anthropic writes the historian’s replies, as section 8 explains. These services may store and process information on servers outside your country, including in the United States.

Donations. The donate links in UCHRONIA and citylayoutguessr go to my page on Ko-fi. If you give something, Ko-fi and whichever payment service you pick there, Stripe or PayPal, take your payment and your details under their own privacy policies. This site never sees your card or account. Ko-fi shows me the name you give, your email address, and any message you leave, and I only use those to keep track of donations and to say thank you.

I don’t share, rent, or sell anything the site collects. There are no ad networks, no tracking pixels, no third-party analytics, and no mailing list. The only way I’d hand anything over is if the law made me.

12. How long I keep things

I keep accounts, profiles, and scores for as long as the game exists, because a leaderboard with a history is kind of the point, or until you ask me to delete them. I keep the game’s visit lines as a running count of how it gets played, and the site’s daily visit totals as a running count of how much it gets read. When I delete an account, everything tied to it goes with it. I keep feedback messages until I’ve read them and don’t need them anymore, and never for longer than a year. UCHRONIA’s daily counts are deleted after thirty days, and nothing else from the historian is kept at all.

You can sign out whenever you want, and you can change your display name from the game’s menu. Beyond that, you can ask me to:

Fix it. Correct anything that’s wrong.
Delete it. Get rid of your account, your profile, and every score attached to it.

Email me at noahlee519@gmail.com from the address you signed in with, so I know it’s really you. I’ll take care of it as soon as I see it, and always within thirty days. You get these rights wherever you live. If you’re somewhere whose law gives them to you formally, like the United Kingdom, the European Union, or California, this is how you use them, and you can also complain to your local data protection authority.

14. Security

The site is served over HTTPS. The game’s database lets a browser add a visit line but never read one back, lets each person post only their own score, and lets only you change your own profile. Nothing is perfectly secure, though. If something ever went wrong that affected your information, I’d tell you.
